# Writing Guidelines — Core Editorial Policy

*Inspired by Stephen King's On Writing, adapted for scientific and technical communication.*

## Purpose

Write so that readers can reconstruct the author's ideas, follow the argument, and evaluate the evidence.

Preserve the author's voice while improving clarity, precision, coherence, and economy.

**Preserve the author's voice. Remove what obscures the idea.**

## 1. Write for a Specific Reader

Before writing, identify the intended reader, their background knowledge, and the question they expect the text to answer.

Provide the information necessary to understand the argument. Do not explain familiar concepts merely to fill space, and do not omit technical details that are necessary for understanding.

Use the reader's anticipated questions to guide the order of explanations.

## 2. Discover the Argument Before Imposing a Structure

Build the narrative from the actual problem, observations, mechanisms, and evidence.

Do not force results into a predetermined story.

Identify what the work establishes, what the results suggest, and what remains unknown.

The order of discovery need not be the order of presentation. Organize the final argument for the reader, without changing what the evidence supports.

The central argument should help determine which details belong in the text. It must not determine which evidence is acceptable.

## 3. Make Each Paragraph Contribute to the Argument

Each paragraph should serve a recognizable purpose: introduce a problem, explain a mechanism, present evidence, interpret a finding, establish a distinction, or identify a limitation.

Allow paragraphs to develop naturally. A paragraph may begin with a claim or lead readers through observations toward one.

Keep supporting information near the claims it supports.

Avoid repeating the same argument merely to create emphasis.

## 4. Prefer Concrete Language

Use specific subjects, verbs, and technical relationships.

Describe what a system does, how it works, and what changes under specified conditions.

Avoid unnecessary nominalizations and abstract evaluations.

Use familiar words for ordinary concepts and precise terminology for technical distinctions.

Do not replace accurate technical expressions with simpler but less precise alternatives.

## 5. Remove Evaluative Language Without Removing Meaning

Review words such as *clearly*, *obviously*, *remarkably*, *greatly*, *effectively*, and *significantly*.

If a modifier merely tells readers how to evaluate a result, remove it or replace it with relevant evidence.

Preserve modifiers that define the scientific claim, including *approximately*, *independently*, *primarily*, and *statistically*.

Treat suspicious expressions as signals for review, not as prohibited vocabulary.

## 6. Distinguish Claims, Mechanisms, and Evidence

Explain the physical, computational, or experimental relationships behind a claim when they are relevant to the argument.

Not every contribution requires a mechanistic explanation. Establishing an empirical relationship or characterizing a phenomenon can be a contribution in its own right.

A claim states what is being argued. A mechanism explains how or why a behavior may occur. Evidence establishes what was observed or measured.

Keep these roles distinct.

Do not treat a plausible mechanism as a verified causal explanation without appropriate evidence.

Do not describe an expected downstream benefit as an established result.

## 7. Preserve Scientific Precision

Distinguish design descriptions, model predictions, simulation results, physical observations, interpretations, and hypotheses.

Retain assumptions, boundary conditions, experimental settings, citations, notation, and meaningful uncertainty.

Match the strength of each claim to its evidence.

Do not invent measurements, citations, comparisons, or explanations to complete a narrative.

Brevity must never come at the expense of accuracy.

## 8. Use Sentence Structure Deliberately

Prefer active voice when it clarifies who or what performs an action. Use passive voice when the object or procedure is the relevant subject.

Review long sentences for unnecessary complexity. Preserve them when they express a relationship more effectively than several short sentences.

Use contrasts, lists, rhetorical questions, and punctuation when they help readers understand the argument.

Do not enforce arbitrary word counts, sentence lengths, or punctuation quotas.

## 9. Revise in Separate Passes

**Discovery draft:** Develop the explanation and identify the central argument without polishing every sentence.

**Structural revision:** Evaluate the argument, evidence, paragraph functions, missing reasoning, and unnecessary material.

**Prose revision:** Improve word choice, sentence construction, transitions, and economy.

**Verification:** Confirm that the revised text preserves scientific claims, numerical information, citations, and uncertainty.

Do not perform extensive sentence-level polishing before the argument is sufficiently settled.

## 10. Edit Without Replacing the Author

Use the author's existing writing as a reference for narrative structure, explanation, technical detail, and terminology.

Prefer the smallest change that materially improves the text.

Do not introduce generic academic language to make the writing sound more sophisticated.

Do not introduce new claims, interpretations, or rhetorical emphasis solely to make the text more persuasive.

When scientific reasoning is incomplete, identify the missing information instead of silently rewriting the claim.

When editing, prioritize:

Scientific and factual accuracy.

Clarity and logical coherence.

Preservation of authorial voice.

Economy of expression.

Stylistic preferences.

## Final Editorial Test

For every sentence, ask:

**What does the reader learn from this sentence that they would lose if it were removed?**

If nothing is lost, consider deleting it.

If essential information remains but is difficult to understand, improve the explanation.

If the sentence is technically precise and serves the argument, keep it, regardless of its length.

**The goal is not to produce shorter writing. The goal is to make the intended meaning easier to understand.**
