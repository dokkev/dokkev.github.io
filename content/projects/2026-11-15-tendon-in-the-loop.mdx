## Motivation

Tendon-driven robots transmit actuator forces through cables, or tendons, whose response depends on deformation, friction, routing, and loading history. Motor motion and torque alone may therefore leave distal tension and displacement uncertain. As the cable mechanics change with repeated use, an estimator must also distinguish those changes from robot motion.

Can motor-side observations reveal both hidden transmission states and changes in cable mechanics over long-term robot operation?

Tendon-in-the-Loop (TiL) proposes a two-motor hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) dynamometer coupled to a MuJoCo finger to investigate this question. The planned workflow uses transmission measurements to identify a physics-based baseline, compare it with learned models, and develop an observer that estimates transmission state and mechanics from motor-side signals. General cable mechanics and MuJoCo tendon representations are covered in the Cable blog series.

## Proposed HIL Testbed

In the proposed testbed, the input quasi-direct-drive (QDD) motor drives the cable, guides or pulleys, and terminations under test. The emulation motor reproduces the distal displacement imposed by a virtual two-degree-of-freedom (2-DoF) finger. Together, the two motors form one physical tendon port. Each additional independent tendon port requires its own actuator pair and instrumentation.

Separate input and output tension measurements, together with motor encoders and measurements of emulation and pulley motion, would characterize force and motion at both transmission boundaries. These measurements allow transmission losses to be evaluated without equating motor torque with distal tendon force.

Measured output tension drives the virtual finger through its tendon length Jacobian:

$$
\boldsymbol\tau_t=-\mathbf J_\ell(\mathbf q)^T T_{\mathrm{out}},
\qquad
\mathbf J_\ell=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf q}.
$$

Positive tension resists tendon lengthening. MuJoCo advances the finger dynamics and contact response, and the resulting configuration sets the emulation command:

$$
x_e^{\mathrm{cmd}}=\ell(\mathbf q)-\ell(\mathbf q_0).
$$

Both actuator coordinates and signs must be calibrated to this tendon port. The finger supplies inertia, passive mechanics, gravity, and contact; transmission force must not be applied again through a duplicate numerical tendon spring or friction model.

### Finger Configuration and Physical Routing

The initial dynamometer design uses fixed physical routing. Changes in the MuJoCo finger configuration change virtual tendon length and moment arms while the physical cable path stays fixed. Routing is therefore a separate experimental condition. A reconfigurable fixture could test selected routes; continuously matching the physical route to finger motion would require additional mechanical motion.

## Planned Long-Horizon Contact Experiments

The planned protocol repeats approaches, presses, holds, releases, and repositioning against objects. Contact can raise tendon tension while constraining joint motion, exposing transmission behavior that free motion alone may not reveal.

Long-duration operation would test mechanical drift and dependence on loading history. The protocol would vary contact location and stiffness, initial finger configuration, motor excitation, pretension, dwell time, cable condition, and physical routing. Repeated reference episodes would help distinguish changes with accumulated use from differences caused by the current contact task.

### State Groups and Data

| Data source | Notation | Available information |
|---|---|---|
| **MuJoCo finger** | $S_{\mathrm{MJ}}$ | Joint position/velocity, tendon geometry, object and contact state |
| **Physical testbed** | $S_{\mathrm{TB}}$ | Motor states, separate input/output tensions, pulley and boundary motion, apparent deformation |
| **Deployment observations** | $\mathbf o_t^M$ | QDD motor position, velocity, current or torque estimate, and command only |

The deployment observations do not include measured finger joint configurations. Interpreting motor current also requires current-to-torque calibration and accounting for actuator losses.

Synchronized records would retain timestamps, units, calibration, routing, and operating history. Apparent deformation $\delta_{\mathrm{app}}$ is computed from calibrated boundary motion. It may include termination slip and structural compliance, so it cannot be interpreted as cable strain alone.

Numerical verification additionally records known plant parameters and internal states in a separate reference namespace. The protocol declares which may supervise training and which remain sealed for evaluation. Physical experiments do not automatically expose these internal quantities. Training, validation, and test runs are disjoint, with operating periods, contact/cable conditions, and routing trials withheld rather than randomly splitting adjacent cycles.

## Transmission Modeling Approaches

Model comparison would test whether learning improves on an identified physics-based transmission model. The subsequent observer study would evaluate whether the identified structure helps recover hidden state from motor observations.

| Approach | Formulation | Comparison purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Physics-based** | Identify effective stiffness, damping, friction, and reference length | Interpretable baseline and parameter-recovery checks |
| **Hybrid** | $\hat T_{\mathrm{out}}=T_{\mathrm{physics}}+\Delta T_{\mathrm{NN}}$ | Whether a causal learned residual improves accuracy or data efficiency |
| **Fully learned** | $\hat T_{\mathrm{out},t}=f_\theta(\mathbf b_{0:t})$ | Whether boundary-history learning generalizes without an explicit mechanics decomposition |

The models would share training and validation runs, with inputs and tuning budgets reported. Here, $\mathbf b_{0:t}$ denotes the history of boundary inputs available to the forward predictor. The output tension at time $t$ would be withheld as an input when predicting that same tension. Any past output measurement used by a forward predictor must also be available in its intended use.

Comparison includes held-out force MAE, loading/unloading response, data efficiency, drift sensitivity, and closed-loop contact response. An open-loop fit to prescribed boundary motion does not establish useful behavior in a coupled robot simulation. Forward prediction and motor-only observation also have different information: an observer cannot assume access to distal boundary motion merely because a transmission predictor uses it.

## Neural State and Mechanics Observer

The proposed observer would estimate hidden transmission state and evolving effective mechanics from motor-side observations:

$$
\mathbf o_t^M=[q_{m,t},\dot q_{m,t},i_{m,t},u_{m,t}].
$$

Here, $q_m$, $\dot q_m$, $i_m$, and $u_m$ are motor position, velocity, current, and command. Candidate state and parameter targets are

$$
\mathbf x_t^{\mathrm{trans}}
=[T_{\mathrm{out},t},\delta_{\mathrm{app},t},x_{\mathrm{out},t},\mathbf z_t],
\qquad
\boldsymbol\theta_t^{\mathrm{trans}}
=[k_{\mathrm{eff},t},\mu_{\mathrm{eff},t},\ell_{\mathrm{slack},t},\ldots].
$$

The state targets include distal tension, apparent deformation, output displacement, and internal memory states $\mathbf z_t$. The parameter targets include effective stiffness, friction, and slack length. A causal observer would update its estimates and recurrent memory $\mathbf h_t$ as

$$
\boxed{
(\hat{\mathbf x}_t^{\mathrm{trans}},\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}_t^{\mathrm{trans}},\mathbf h_{t+1})
=F_\eta(\mathbf o_t^M,\mathbf h_t).
}
$$

A model-informed observer would use the identified transmission structure to estimate the subset of states and effective parameters supported by the measurements. An end-to-end observer could instead encode changing mechanics in recurrent memory $\mathbf h_t$ without explicit parameter outputs. Hysteresis memory evolves as a state; latent coordinates require an identification reference before they can be interpreted as measured stiffness or friction.

Testbed measurements would supervise training, while deployment would use motor-side channels only. Distal tension, apparent deformation, and output displacement can be evaluated against testbed measurements. Parameter recovery can use known numerical references during verification; physical mechanics estimates need independent calibration or identification evidence.

### Observability and Robot-Level Use

One tendon displacement does not generally determine both $q_1$ and $q_2$, and different combinations of stiffness, slack, and friction can produce similar motor histories. The observer must report uncertainty and failure conditions rather than assume unique state recovery. Joint estimation requires specified mechanical coupling, a sufficiently constrained dynamic observer, or additional contact information.

Fingertip-force inference further requires a usable configuration estimate, contact location and directions, and accounting for passive, gravitational, and inertial torques. A single tension does not determine an arbitrary contact wrench. Independent joint sensing and a calibrated fingertip force sensor would evaluate predictions in physical-finger experiments without becoming inputs to the deployed observer.

## Validation Strategy

Verification starts with **Synthetic Data Verification**: an independent numerical transmission plant supplies contact-rich runs for coupling checks and an initial motor-only observer study. Known internal states provide references before physical implementation. Parameter identification and broader model comparison remain to be evaluated; physical HIL validation is a later stage.

### Evidence and Acceptance Criteria

| Experiment | Quantitative evidence | Success / failure decision |
|---|---|---|
| **Force–motion coupling** | Emulator position RMSE/peak error, phase lag over excitation frequency, port work and stored/dissipated energy balance | Compare with specified tracking, latency, tension, and energy tolerances; flag sustained oscillation, saturation, or timestep-sensitive response |
| **Physics-based system ID** | Parameter error against known numerical references; held-out tension MAE/RMSE in N | Recovery must be supported by excitation and uncertainty; correlated or non-unique fits remain unresolved |
| **Hybrid vs. fully learned** | Force MAE, learning curves against dataset size, unseen-contact error and closed-loop response | Added complexity must improve the task-relevant held-out metric under a stated data/tuning budget |
| **Long-term mechanics** | State/parameter error versus operating time and known or independently measured drift | Check whether drift is tracked within the task tolerance; report loss of calibration and confounded changes |
| **Motor-only observer** | Distal tension error in N, deformation/displacement error in mm, uncertainty coverage | Use only motor-side inputs; identify regimes exceeding the estimation tolerance or unsupported by observability |
| **Fingertip force** | Force-vector MAE/RMSE against an independent sensor, paired difference from the calibrated QDD baseline | Compare under the same contact conditions and force target; report ambiguity or degradation as well as improvement |

Numerical acceptance limits will be set before evaluation using motor and emulator bandwidth, working tension range, sensor resolution, and the control task. Simulation solver guards and unit-test tolerances are not hardware acceptance specifications. Settings will be selected using calibration and validation data, with final test data held out.

### Synthetic Data Verification

The local MuJoCo prototype includes an independent two-segment cable/pulley plant, QDD and emulation motor models, and a coupled contact scenario.

The verification was executed with MuJoCo **4.0.1**, Python **3.13.5**, and NumPy **2.5.5**. The current suite passes **40 mechanical, coupling, data-pipeline, and observer-causality tests**. Five three-cycle cable presets, 26 sensitivity cases, and a 50-cycle drift-law run were also executed.

| Check | Observed numerical result |
|---|---|
| Nonlinear baseline, 3 cycles at 0.5 Hz | Emulator RMSE **0.0444 mm**; fundamental phase lag **0.127°** |
| Added delay: 0 / 2 / 10 / 30 ms | Peak tracking error **0.814 / 1.544 / 10.791 / 14.534 mm** |
| Current limits: 1 and 2 A | **No recorded contact**, despite numerical completion |
| Drift preset: 50 cycles, 75.3 s | Recorded segment stiffness **10,000 → 8,309 N/m**; creep state **0.0874 mm** |
| Taut viscous plant, 2 s at 3 Hz | Energy-accounting residual **1.19 × 10⁻⁷ J**, or **0.0654%** of port work |

#### Initial Motor-Only Observer

A **32-unit GRU** estimates distal tension and apparent deformation from motor position, velocity, current, and command at **200 Hz**. Its causal memory uses present and past motor observations. Distal measurements and plant states supply targets only; they never enter the observer input. This first study evaluates transmission states, not explicit stiffness or friction estimates.

The dataset contains **24 independent runs**: 12 training, 3 validation, and 9 test runs. Training uses 128-sample windows contained within individual runs; normalization and linear-compliance calibration use training runs only. Validation selects the checkpoint from 60 epochs. Each test starts with reset recurrent memory and is evaluated as a continuous run, including initialization. One training seed was used.

The comparisons are a calibrated QDD torque estimate and a QDD estimate corrected for actuator inertia and viscous losses, paired with a fitted linear-compliance relation for deformation. The compliance fit is an effective baseline, not an identified cable material property. The table reports **baseline → GRU** MAE, averaged equally over three runs per test set; the baseline shown is the dynamics-corrected estimate.

| Held-out test | Distal tension MAE [N] | Apparent deformation MAE [mm] |
|---|---|---|
| Unseen object stiffness / placement | **0.790 → 0.423** | **0.189 → 0.040** |
| Changed friction law and cubic stiffness | **0.702 → 0.467** | **0.348 → 0.261** |
| Accelerated drift, 20 cycles per run | **0.835 → 0.439** | **0.154 → 0.089** |

The changed-law test replaces Dahl friction with smoothed Coulomb friction and reduces cubic stiffness; the drift test introduces creep and degradation absent from training. These stress tests expose sensitivity beyond the training plant settings. Three runs per test set and one training seed do not establish broad generalization, physical transfer, or unique recovery of evolving mechanics. Uncertainty calibration and independent hardware measurements remain necessary.
