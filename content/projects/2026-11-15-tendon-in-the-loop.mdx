## Motivation

Tendon-driven robots transmit actuator forces through flexible cables whose behavior depends on routing geometry, deformation, friction, and loading history. These mechanisms introduce three coupled challenges.

**Configuration-dependent dynamics.** Tendon routing determines how motor motion and force reach the joints. As a finger moves, changes in cable contact, bending, and moment arms can alter the transmission response.

**Time-varying dynamics.** Repeated loading can change effective cable length, stiffness, friction, and hysteresis through settling, creep, and wear. A model identified under one mechanical condition may no longer describe the transmission after extended operation.

**Partial observability.** Motor position, velocity, and current do not directly reveal distal tendon tension or joint configuration. Cable deformation and friction obscure the relationship between actuator motion and the state of the finger.

These challenges are coupled in a functioning robot. Joint motion changes tendon loading and potentially its routing. Loading history changes the transmission response, while uncertainty in that response makes joint motion and contact difficult to infer from motor measurements.

For example, when a motor moves but a finger joint moves less than expected, the discrepancy may come from cable elongation, transmission friction, or external contact. Several effects can produce similar motor-side measurements, making their individual contributions difficult to identify from experiments on the assembled hand.

**The problem is not only to model nonlinear tendon dynamics, but to distinguish their contributions when transmission mechanics, operating history, and hidden robot states interact.**

*Tendon-in-the-Loop (TiL)* proposes a hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) dynamometer that couples an instrumented tendon transmission to a virtual robotic finger in MuJoCo. The testbed separates the physical transmission from the robot dynamics while preserving their force–motion interaction. Robot loading and contact conditions can be controlled independently of selected transmission properties, allowing their effects to be examined before they are combined.

The long-term objective is to estimate **distal tendon tension and finger joint positions from motor-side measurements**, despite nonlinear and evolving transmission mechanics. System identification and adaptive modeling provide ways to characterize the transmission, while state observers investigate what can be recovered without distal sensing.

The central question is

**Can motor-side measurements support reliable estimation of distal tendon tension and joint states under configuration-dependent, time-varying, and partially observed transmission dynamics?**

General cable mechanics and MuJoCo tendon representations are discussed in the Cable blog series.

## Proposed HIL Testbed

The proposed dynamometer connects two actuators through the cable, guides or pulleys, and terminations under test.

An input quasi-direct-drive (QDD) motor drives the tendon as it would in a robotic finger. An emulation motor reproduces the distal boundary motion generated by a virtual two-degree-of-freedom (2-DoF) finger. Together, the actuators form one tendon transmission channel. Each additional independently tested tendon requires another actuator pair and corresponding instrumentation.

Separate input and output load cells measure tension at both ends of the transmission. Motor encoders and pulley motion measurements provide the corresponding kinematic information. Measuring both boundaries allows transmission losses and deformation to be characterized without assuming that motor torque directly determines distal tendon force.

### Force–Motion Coupling

The physical transmission and virtual finger exchange force and motion.

Measured distal tendon tension is applied to the simulated finger through its tendon length Jacobian.

$$
\boldsymbol\tau_t
=
-\mathbf J_\ell(\mathbf q)^T T_{\mathrm{out}},
\qquad
\mathbf J_\ell
=
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mathbf q}.
$$

Here, $\ell(\mathbf q)$ is the tendon length determined by the finger configuration. Positive tension resists tendon lengthening.

MuJoCo advances the joint dynamics, including inertia, passive mechanics, gravity, and object contact. The updated configuration determines the emulation motor command.

$$
x_e^{\mathrm{cmd}}
=
\ell(\mathbf q)-\ell(\mathbf q_0).
$$

Actuator coordinates and signs must be calibrated consistently with the tendon displacement convention. The physical transmission supplies the tendon force, so its stiffness and friction must not be applied again through a duplicate numerical tendon model.

This coupling allows the transmission to experience loading generated by robot motion and contact, while the virtual finger responds to the force actually transmitted through the cable.

### Finger Configuration and Physical Routing

A compact tendon-driven hand can have cable contact conditions that change as its joints move. Reproducing those conditions in a dynamometer is difficult because the cable path is constrained by the finger mechanism.

The initial TiL design uses a fixed physical route. MuJoCo joint motion changes virtual tendon length and moment arms, but does not automatically change the physical cable path inside the dynamometer.

Physical routing is therefore treated as a separate experimental condition. Interchangeable fixtures could characterize selected bending radii, wrap angles, and guide arrangements. Reproducing a continuously changing route would require additional mechanical motion synchronized with the virtual finger.

The initial apparatus can characterize transmission behavior under robot-generated boundary loading, but does not reproduce every moving contact interaction inside an articulated finger.

## Experimental Methodology

### Controlled Contact and Loading

The planned experiments repeatedly move the virtual finger toward an object, establish contact, press, hold, release, and reposition.

Contact is useful for transmission characterization because joint motion can become constrained while tendon tension continues to change. The resulting response may expose compliance, friction, and loading-history effects that are less apparent during free motion.

The experiments are organized to distinguish three sources of uncertainty before evaluating their combined effects.

**Transmission dynamics** are studied by varying motor excitation, pretension, contact location, object stiffness, initial finger configuration, and physical routing. Controlled comparisons reveal how loading and routing affect the relationship between input motion, distal tension, and finger motion.

**Time-varying mechanics** are studied through repeated operation over extended periods. Cable settling, creep, and changes in effective stiffness or friction may alter the transmission response even when the commanded motion remains the same. Periodically repeated reference trajectories help separate changes associated with accumulated use from differences caused by the current task.

**Partial observability** is studied by restricting the estimator to motor-side signals while retaining independent transmission measurements and simulated joint states as references. Experiments under different excitation and contact conditions reveal where hidden states can be estimated and where different physical states remain difficult to distinguish.

The final experiments combine changing transmission properties, operating history, and robot contact conditions. This tests whether an identified model and state observer remain useful when the effects occur together.

The aim is not simply to collect more data. It is to establish which transmission effects can be identified, which hidden states can be estimated, and which ambiguities remain under motor-side sensing.

### Measurements and Data

The experimental framework separates robot state, transmission measurements, and the information available to a deployed observer.

| Data source | Notation | Available information |
|---|---|---|
| **MuJoCo finger** | $S_{\mathrm{MJ}}$ | Joint position and velocity, tendon geometry, object and contact state |
| **Instrumented testbed** | $S_{\mathrm{TB}}$ | Motor states, input/output tension, pulley and boundary motion, apparent deformation |
| **Motor-side observations** | $\mathbf o_t^M$ | QDD motor position, velocity, current or torque estimate, and command |

These sources serve different purposes.

MuJoCo provides the reference joint states and contact conditions of the virtual finger. The instrumented testbed measures how the transmission responds to those conditions. The deployed observer receives only motor-side signals, without joint encoders or distal tension measurements.

Apparent transmission deformation, $\delta_{\mathrm{app}}$, is derived from calibrated boundary motion. It may include cable stretch, termination slip, and structural compliance, so it is not automatically equivalent to cable material strain.

Synchronized records retain timestamps, units, actuator calibration, routing conditions, and operating history. Numerical verification additionally records known transmission parameters and internal states in a separate reference group. Those internal quantities are available because the numerical model defines them; physical experiments do not automatically provide equivalent ground truth.

Training, validation, and test runs are separated by operating period, contact conditions, and cable or routing configurations rather than randomly splitting adjacent loading cycles. The protocol specifies which privileged channels may supervise training and which remain reserved for evaluation.

Before physical implementation, an independent numerical transmission model replaces the physical tendon in the same coupling architecture. This allows the force–motion interface, data pipeline, and initial estimation methods to be tested under known mechanical conditions.

## Transmission Modeling

The testbed is intended to identify compact transmission models that remain useful under robot-generated loading.

The first step is to fit an effective physics-based model using measured input/output motion and tension. Candidate parameters include stiffness, damping, friction, and reference length. These are parameters of the chosen reduced-order model and need not correspond uniquely to material properties.

The identified model provides a baseline for evaluating whether learned dynamics improve prediction or generalization.

| Approach | Formulation | Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Physics-based identification** | Effective stiffness, damping, friction, and reference length | How much behavior can a compact mechanical model explain? |
| **Physics + learned residual** | $\hat T_{\mathrm{out}}=T_{\mathrm{physics}}+\Delta T_{\mathrm{NN}}$ | Does learning the unexplained response improve accuracy or data efficiency? |
| **Fully learned dynamics** | $\hat T_{\mathrm{out},t}=f_\theta(\mathbf b_{0:t})$ | Can boundary-motion and loading history predict transmission behavior without an explicit mechanics decomposition? |

The models use the same identification and validation runs, with their input signals and tuning budgets reported.

Here, $\mathbf b_{0:t}$ denotes the history of boundary signals available to a forward transmission model. The output tension being predicted is excluded as a contemporaneous input. This distinction matters because a forward transmission model may use known distal boundary motion, while a deployed motor-only observer cannot assume that motion is measured.

Evaluation includes force prediction error, loading/unloading response, data efficiency, sensitivity to mechanical drift, and performance under held-out contact or routing conditions.

An accurate fit to prescribed boundary trajectories is not sufficient by itself. The identified model must also be tested when coupled to robot dynamics, where errors in transmission force can alter motion and contact.

Comparisons across routing configurations examine whether a shared model structure remains valid with different parameters or whether changes in cable contact require a different model. Such transfer cannot be assumed from performance on a single fixed route.

## Motor-Side State Estimation

Transmission modeling describes how the cable responds to boundary conditions. The observer addresses a different question — whether the hidden distal state can be reconstructed when only motor-side measurements are available.

The deployed observation is

$$
\mathbf o_t^M
=
[q_{m,t},\dot q_{m,t},i_{m,t},u_{m,t}],
$$

where the terms represent QDD motor position, velocity, current, and command. Motor current must be interpreted through an appropriate torque calibration and actuator loss model.

The two primary estimation targets are distal tendon tension and finger joint position.

$$
\boxed{
\hat{\mathbf y}_t
=
[\hat T_{\mathrm{out},t},\hat q_{1,t},\hat q_{2,t}]
}
$$

A causal observer uses the current motor observation and its accumulated history.

$$
(\hat{\mathbf y}_t,\mathbf h_{t+1})
=
F_\eta(\mathbf o_t^M,\mathbf h_t).
$$

The recurrent state $\mathbf h_t$ carries information about previous motion and loading. Auxiliary outputs may include apparent deformation, distal displacement, and estimates of evolving transmission mechanics.

For a model-informed observer, the effective mechanics parameters may be written as

$$
\boldsymbol\theta_t^{\mathrm{trans}}
=
[k_{\mathrm{eff},t},
\mu_{\mathrm{eff},t},
\ell_{\mathrm{slack},t},\ldots].
$$

These parameters describe a selected transmission model. Their physical interpretation and identifiability require separate verification.

A fully learned observer may instead represent changes in transmission mechanics implicitly through latent memory. In either case, training can use privileged testbed measurements, while inference remains restricted to the motor-side observations defined above.

### Observability and Mechanical Adaptation

Joint position estimation is more difficult than distal tension prediction because tendon deformation and joint motion contribute to the same motor displacement.

For a compliant transmission,

$$
x_m=\ell(q_1,q_2)+\delta,
$$

where $\delta$ represents effective transmission deformation under a consistent displacement convention.

Even if $\delta$ were known, one tendon length does not generally determine two independent joint angles. Mechanical coupling, dynamic constraints, contact conditions, and excitation history may provide additional information, but unique reconstruction cannot be assumed.

Time-varying mechanics introduce another ambiguity. An observed change in motor response may reflect a change in cable stiffness or slack, a different contact condition, or a different joint state.

The observer must therefore be evaluated not only by prediction error but also by the conditions under which its estimates remain distinguishable. Uncertainty and failure cases are part of that evaluation.

The proposed study compares model-informed and learned observers, including approaches that adapt their internal mechanics representation over time. Whether adaptation can recover from previously unseen cable conditions remains an experimental question.

### Robot-Level Force Estimation

Distal tendon tension provides a more direct estimate of transmitted force than uncorrected motor torque, but it does not alone determine fingertip contact force.

Mapping tendon tension to joint torque requires tendon geometry and joint configuration. Inferring a fingertip wrench additionally requires the relevant contact location and direction, together with passive, gravitational, and inertial contributions.

A later physical-finger experiment would compare transmission-aware estimation against calibrated QDD-based estimation. Independent joint measurements and a calibrated fingertip force sensor would provide evaluation references, not inputs to the deployed observer.

## Validation Strategy

Validation proceeds from numerical verification of the coupled architecture to identification and observation experiments, followed by physical transmission and robot-level evaluation.

The experiments address distinct questions before testing their combined effects.

| Experiment | Evidence | Evaluation question |
|---|---|---|
| **Force–motion coupling** | Emulator tracking error, phase lag, port energy balance, stability under delay and saturation | Does the testbed reproduce the commanded boundary motion without unacceptable numerical or control behavior? |
| **Transmission identification** | Parameter error where identifiable, held-out tension MAE/RMSE | Can the measured response support a compact predictive model? |
| **Model comparison** | Force prediction, hysteresis response, data efficiency, closed-loop contact response | Does additional model complexity improve prediction under comparable conditions? |
| **Time-varying mechanics** | Error over operating history, adaptation response, known or independently measured drift | Does the model remain calibrated as the transmission changes? |
| **Motor-side observation** | Distal tension MAE, joint-angle MAE, deformation error, uncertainty | Which hidden quantities can be estimated without distal sensing? |
| **Robot-level evaluation** | Fingertip force error against independent sensing, comparison with QDD baseline | Does transmission-aware estimation improve force prediction under stated contact assumptions? |

Numerical acceptance limits must be distinguished from hardware requirements. Physical tracking, latency, tension, and estimation tolerances will depend on actuator bandwidth, sensor resolution, operating range, and the intended control task.

Calibration and model selection use training and validation data. Final evaluation uses disjoint test runs, including mechanical conditions or operating histories not used during fitting.

## Synthetic Data Verification

An initial numerical prototype implements the proposed coupling architecture using an independent two-segment cable/pulley plant, QDD motor dynamics, an emulation motor model, and a MuJoCo finger interacting with objects.

This prototype tests the integration and estimation methods before physical hardware is available. Its constitutive laws and internal states are defined numerically rather than measured from a real cable.

### Force–Motion Coupling

The verification was executed with MuJoCo **4.0.1**, Python **3.13.5**, and NumPy **2.5.5**.

The current implementation passes **40 mechanical, coupling, data-pipeline, and observer-causality tests**. Additional numerical experiments include five three-cycle cable presets, 26 sensitivity cases, and a 50-cycle drift-law run.

| Numerical experiment | Observed result |
|---|---|
| Nonlinear baseline, 3 cycles at 0.5 Hz | Emulator RMSE **0.0444 mm**; fundamental phase lag **0.127°** |
| Added delay of 0 / 2 / 10 / 30 ms | Peak tracking error **0.814 / 1.544 / 10.791 / 14.534 mm** |
| Current limits of 1 and 2 A | **No recorded contact**, despite numerical completion |
| Drift preset, 50 cycles over 75.3 s | Prescribed segment stiffness **10,000 → 8,309 N/m**; creep state **0.0874 mm** |
| Taut viscous plant, 2 s at 3 Hz | Energy-accounting residual **1.19 × 10⁻⁷ J**, equivalent to **0.0654%** of port work |

These tests expose operating conditions that require attention during hardware design.

The 10 and 30 ms delay cases and the 0.02 m/s emulation velocity limit triggered the script's oscillation diagnostic. Its 5 mm warning threshold is a numerical diagnostic, not a hardware acceptance criterion.

A near-rigid, ideal-emulator preset reached **408 N** peak tension. Finite numerical states therefore do not imply that the corresponding physical system would operate safely.

The reported phase lag describes one cycle's fundamental component rather than the full emulator bandwidth. Energy accounting was checked only for the stated viscous case. The stiffness and creep values are prescribed states of the synthetic degradation model, not identified cable material properties.

### Initial Motor-Only Observer

The first estimation experiment evaluates whether a causal neural observer can infer transmission state from QDD measurements.

A **32-unit GRU** receives motor position, velocity, current, and command at **200 Hz**. It predicts distal tendon tension and apparent transmission deformation. Distal measurements and numerical plant states provide training targets but are excluded from the model inputs.

This experiment does **not yet evaluate joint-position estimation or explicit identification of evolving stiffness and friction**.

The dataset contains **24 independent runs**, divided into 12 training, 3 validation, and 9 test runs.

Training uses 128-sample windows that remain within individual runs. Input normalization and linear-compliance calibration use training data only. The validation set selects a checkpoint from 60 epochs. Each test run starts with reset recurrent memory and is evaluated continuously, including initialization. Results are from one training seed.

The GRU is compared against a calibrated QDD torque estimate and a dynamics-corrected QDD estimate that accounts for actuator inertia and viscous losses. A fitted linear-compliance relation provides the deformation baseline.

The following table reports the dynamics-corrected baseline and GRU errors. Each test group contains three independent runs, weighted equally.

| Held-out condition | Distal tension MAE [N] | Apparent deformation MAE [mm] |
|---|---|---|
| Unseen object stiffness / placement | **0.790 → 0.423** | **0.189 → 0.040** |
| Changed friction law and cubic stiffness | **0.702 → 0.467** | **0.348 → 0.261** |
| Accelerated drift, 20 cycles per run | **0.835 → 0.439** | **0.154 → 0.089** |

The GRU reduces tension and deformation error relative to the selected baseline in all three test groups. The unseen-contact test gives the lowest deformation error at **0.040 mm**, while the changed-law test increases it to **0.261 mm**.

The changed-law experiment replaces Dahl friction with smoothed Coulomb friction and reduces cubic stiffness. The accelerated-drift experiment introduces creep and degradation absent from training. These tests examine sensitivity to mechanical changes outside the training conditions, but do not establish generalization to arbitrary cable mechanics.

The results are limited by three test runs per condition, one training seed, and a numerically defined reference plant. They also do not establish whether the model has identified the underlying mechanical changes or has learned a useful predictive relationship within the tested conditions.

### Current Scope

The numerical experiments establish a reproducible environment for testing force–motion coupling and an initial motor-only transmission observer. They also show that accurate prediction under one set of contact conditions does not establish equal accuracy when the transmission law changes.

The central questions remain open. How much of the transmission dynamics can be identified across different routing geometries? Can an observer distinguish mechanical aging from changes in robot loading? Under which conditions can distal tension and joint positions be reconstructed from motor-side measurements alone?

These questions motivate the proposed physical HIL experiments, where controlled transmission measurements can test the assumptions that numerical models cannot validate independently.
