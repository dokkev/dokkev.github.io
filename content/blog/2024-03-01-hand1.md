## Designing a Hand for Physical Interaction

A robotic hand has to fit several fingers, actuators, transmissions, and sensors into a limited volume while producing the forces needed for manipulation. Its design also determines how contact forces reach the actuators and sensors. This post focuses on those hardware choices.

## What Is a Robotic Hand?

People often use “robotic hand” and “robotic gripper” interchangeably because both grasp and manipulate objects. Degrees of freedom (DoF) alone do not settle the distinction. Here I use “robotic hand” for a human-inspired end-effector with multiple fingers, designed for a range of contact interactions.

A gripper may be designed around a narrower set of tasks, such as picking, sorting, or stacking. A hand may also need to support:

- **Non-prehensile manipulation:** flicking, tapping, or pushing objects.
- **In-hand manipulation:** rotating or shifting an object within the hand.
- **Tactile interaction:** using contact to estimate properties such as texture or shape.
- **Multi-contact support:** holding a railing to help stabilize the robot during locomotion.

These capabilities are not exclusive to hands, and a hand-shaped mechanism does not automatically provide them. The distinction describes the intended range of interaction, rather than a strict classification of every device.

My working definition is:

> A human-inspired robotic end-effector composed of multiple kinematic chains (fingers), designed to apply force and motion to the environment and receive contact feedback through its mechanics and sensors.

## Choosing the End-Effector for the Task

A hand with many controlled joints is not necessary for every manipulation task. A one- or two-DoF gripper can be sufficient for a constrained pick-and-place task. Suction grippers offer another way to hold objects with a suitable sealing surface. Adaptive grippers use compliant materials or mechanisms to conform to different shapes with fewer independent control inputs.

Tasks such as in-hand reorientation can require independently changing several contacts. More controlled joints provide more ways to do that, but also require additional actuation, sensing, and coordination. The useful question is which contacts and motions the task requires, rather than how closely the mechanism resembles a human hand.

## Force–Motion Transparency as a Design Goal

Robotic hands need to be compact and light while producing grasping force. One approach uses small motors with high-ratio reducers; tendons and linkages can transmit their motion to fingers while accommodating space constraints. Those choices also affect how the hand responds when the environment pushes back.

Bhatia et al. [1] examine this relationship through the Direct Drive Hand (DDHand). Their design prioritizes force–motion transparency: transmitting force and motion signals between the actuators and the environment in both directions. The paper describes a four-DoF direct-drive hand and demonstrates a “smack and snatch” grasp that reacts to contact with a table. This demonstration supports that design in the tested task; it does not establish direct drive as the best choice for every hand.

This returns to the design question: what interactions should the hand support, and how should its mechanics transmit the forces and motion involved?
