Learning a dexterous manipulation policy means learning how actions change contact with an object. An object rotation can require one finger to establish support, another to move along the surface, and a third to release. The hand's design shapes which transitions are possible, how precisely they must be commanded, and what the policy can observe.

One route to broader capability expands the conditions covered during training. [Learning Dexterous In-Hand Manipulation](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00177) randomized physical properties to transfer policies from simulation to a real hand. More recently, [Dex4D](https://dex4d.github.io/) trained a policy across more than 3,000 simulated objects. These results motivate a complementary design question: **how should we build the robot so that a policy has less contact complexity to learn and control?**

**Contact Morphology Design (CMD) is my research perspective on designing how contacts form, carry load, change, and release. It connects contact geometry, material distribution, and structural support to the contact transitions a manipulation policy needs.** The aim is to reduce the precision, experience, and adaptation required to realize those transitions. Whether a particular design achieves that reduction is an empirical question.

## From passive adaptation to intentional contact transitions

Mechanical intelligence and passive adaptability show how a body can help organize interaction. For example, [RBO Hand 2](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss10/p18.pdf) uses compliant, underactuated mechanics to realize diverse grasp postures through hand–object interaction. This establishes a useful starting point: mechanical design can take on part of the work otherwise assigned to actuation and control.

Dexterous manipulation also requires changing which contacts exist while preserving enough support to continue the task. A contact that securely holds an object must eventually permit release or repositioning. Deformation that accommodates uncertain approach can also affect where contact occurs, when it releases, and how those events appear to a sensor.

My research focuses on **designing the ability to make and break the contacts needed for dexterous manipulation**. That requires studying contact formation and release alongside load transmission. Softness is one design variable; the target is a set of contact transitions that can be intentionally produced and recognized.

Contact transitions are already studied in control. For instance, [contact-implicit MPC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.18897) generates in-hand contact plans without prescribing a contact sequence in advance. The question I pursue concerns the physical interface itself: how should its morphology be designed so useful transitions are accessible to a controller or learned policy?

## Design the morphology and its control abstraction together

A policy receives observations and issues actions. Between them lies a changing contact interface: distributed forces, deformation, friction, and contact boundaries. The research challenge is to identify which features of that interaction must be represented for the task, and design the interface so that representation remains useful.

For example, a policy could reason about establishing support, shifting a loaded contact, or releasing it. Such an abstraction needs physical meaning. An action intended to release must reliably change support; observations must let the policy distinguish completion from continued attachment or loss of the object. Under partial observation, the representation may need history or uncertainty about contact state.

**The grand challenge is to connect how contact physically forms to an abstraction through which a policy can predict and control its transitions.** A label such as “in contact” is insufficient if states with that label respond very differently to the same action. A useful abstraction must preserve the differences that matter for the next manipulation step.

| Design question | Consequence for the policy |
| --- | --- |
| Over what range of approach errors does a useful contact form? | Determines the precision needed to establish support. |
| How does contact carry load while fingers or the object move? | Determines which support changes the policy can execute without losing the object. |
| What motion releases contact, and how does deformation recover? | Determines the actions and timing needed for release and recontact. |
| Which contact changes can the sensors distinguish? | Determines what the policy can infer and whether its state representation is adequate. |

The working hypothesis is that morphology can make some of these transitions more consistent across object poses and disturbances. That could reduce the variation a policy must learn while preserving the contact transitions the task requires. Morphology can also introduce memory, delayed recovery, or ambiguous observations. Those effects must be evaluated together rather than inferred from compliance alone.

## One outer shape, three internal structures

Two fingertips can use the same soft material and still transform the same motion into different contact forces. An embedded rigid insert redirects load, while internal channels give material room to deform. This example isolates one part of the CMD question: how internal structure changes the contact response to the same commanded motion. The outer envelope and soft material law remain fixed.

- **Hybrid Structure:** a T-shaped rigid carrier extends into the soft pad. The material fills the space around the stem and is bonded to it, with no lateral void.
- **Compliant Structure:** fourteen hexagonal channels form a honeycomb interior. Its walls and outer skin deform above a rigid backing.
- **Solid Structure:** the same outer envelope is filled with soft material and bonded to the backing, without an internal stem.

Internal material volume and mass differ. These are illustrative specimens, with material parameters that have not been fitted to a particular silicone.

Newton computes their deformation under three prescribed platen motions. Each structure receives the same motion in each case, so the resulting forces can differ.

| Case | Prescribed motion | Design question illustrated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal load | Indent from 0 to 2.4 mm over 1.2 s | How does motion become force, and where is that force transmitted? |
| Cyclic load | Two 0 → 2.4 → 0 mm cycles, each lasting 2 s | How does the response change during loading and release? |
| Lateral load | Preload to 1.5 mm, then translate 3 mm sideways | How does the loaded structure respond to shear and redistribute contact? |

All three soft bodies use the same stable Neo-Hookean law, with $E=100$ kPa and $\nu=0.35$. Here, *hyperelastic* names the material model. Changing force slope can also arise from evolving contact geometry and structural constraints; these curves do not isolate those contributions.

The platen friction coefficient is $\mu=0.6$ in every case. Backing and insert surfaces remain fixed. The lateral case reaches its 1.5 mm preload over 0.6 s, then translates over 2 s. The simulation includes numerical damping and omits cell-wall self-contact after collapse. It does not establish calibrated silicone behavior, durability, or hardware control performance.

## What the example exposes to a policy

Normal loading probes how approach becomes force and a supporting region. The cyclic case probes the path through loading, release, and recontact. Lateral motion probes how an existing contact changes under shear. These are components of a manipulation sequence, examined here with prescribed motion and a fixed backing.

A policy representation must distinguish contacts that occupy similar areas but respond differently to the next action. Similar areas can accompany different forces, and a current contact image does not specify how deformation will recover. A candidate abstraction must be tested by whether it predicts the effect of subsequent actions. For these structures, that means relating contact formation and release to the commanded motion, loading history, and available observations.

<details>
<summary>Numerical results for the prescribed loading cases</summary>

At the end of normal loading, all specimens have the same 2.4 mm indentation:

| Structure | Normal reaction | Geometric contact-area estimate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hybrid Structure | 6.344 N | 204.9 mm² |
| Compliant Structure | 1.159 N | 182.2 mm² |
| Solid Structure | 3.980 N | 193.3 mm² |

The embedded stem constrains deformation inside the hybrid pad; the honeycomb leaves room for cellular walls to deform. Under this loading condition, the filled pad lies between their normal-force responses.

A requirement to limit force over an uncertain approach would favor a lower force rise over that approach range. A requirement to carry a specified load within limited travel would ask whether the structure reaches that load before exhausting its travel. The endpoint values above cannot settle either choice: both require the response over the relevant motion range. Contact-region evolution adds where the structure transmits load as that motion proceeds.

Loading history also belongs in the requirement. The cyclic case computes loading and return motion. Its friction and numerical damping dissipate energy, so a loading–return difference does not establish a physical material's viscoelasticity or fatigue behavior. A repeated-contact design would need recovery evaluated on its actual cycle and timescale.

The lateral case holds indentation at 1.5 mm and moves the platen sideways. At 3 mm translation:

| Structure | Normal reaction | Lateral reaction | Geometric contact-area estimate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hybrid Structure | 3.152 N | -1.873 N | 161.9 mm² |
| Compliant Structure | 0.554 N | -0.332 N | 140.4 mm² |
| Solid Structure | 1.950 N | -1.167 N | 147.4 mm² |

Negative lateral reaction opposes the platen's positive translation. The shared friction coefficient means these differences cannot be attributed to different surface friction properties. Normal preload forces and deformation differ across the structures. For a task requiring shear transmission, the next comparison must specify whether indentation or normal force is held equal. The contact view alone does not classify sticking and sliding.

</details>

## Test whether design reduces the learning burden

The animation contains prescribed motions and mechanical responses. It does not test intentional multi-finger contact switching, sensor-based state estimation, or a learned manipulation policy. The next research step is to connect candidate morphologies to those capabilities.

A learning-based evaluation should ask how much training each design needs to reach a specified task performance. It should also compare performance at equal training budgets and test object poses or disturbances outside the training distribution. The task, observation access, policy architecture, and training protocol must be stated so a mechanical benefit can be distinguished from a training change.

The contact abstraction needs its own evaluation. Can the available observations identify the state needed for the next action? Does the same abstract action produce a consistent transition across the intended conditions? A simpler representation is useful only if it retains the information needed for successful manipulation.

My research goal is to design the physical contact process and the representation used to control it together. The decisive experiment is a manipulation sequence that requires repeated support changes: measure whether a morphology makes those transitions more reliable, and whether that reliability reduces the data or training variation a policy needs.
