When we talk about physical interaction in robotics, we usually talk about force. A robot pushes with 10 N. A gripper applies 20 N. A controller tracks a desired wrench.

These descriptions are useful. To see what they leave out, let's follow how contact develops.

Imagine a robot finger pressing against an object. The outer surface touches first. As the finger keeps moving, the soft material deforms and a contact patch forms. With more preload, the patch grows and its effective stiffness changes. Load spreads over the surface, travels through the soft material into the rigid structure, becomes joint torque, passes through the transmission, and reaches the actuator. The actuator responds, the finger moves, and the contact changes again.

Contact is part of a closed physical loop:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{actuation}\rightarrow\text{structure}\rightarrow\text{deformation}\\
&\quad\rightarrow\text{contact}\rightarrow\text{load transmission}\\
&\quad\rightarrow\text{structure}\rightarrow\text{actuation}.
\end{aligned}
$$

In a robot, that loop is wired into another one:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{sensing}\rightarrow\text{model}\rightarrow\text{controller}\\
&\quad\rightarrow\text{hardware}\rightarrow\text{contact}\rightarrow\text{sensing}.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the perspective behind what I call **Contact Morphology Design**, or **CMD**.

## Force alone does not describe contact

When two bodies touch, geometry, material deformation, friction, structural support, and motion all shape what happens. What we call a contact force is one useful summary of that richer process.

Robot control works with abstractions such as $q$, $\dot q$, $\tau$, $F$, and $W$. That is what makes control tractable. But every abstraction hides information.

A normal force of 10 N does not say whether the contact patch is large or small. It does not locate pressure concentrations, reveal internal collapse, or describe patch migration and proximity to slip. Two fingertips can produce the same measured force with different pressure distributions and deformations.

If those differences matter for the task, the robot needs information beyond the measured force. Tactile sensing, contact-location estimates, deformation models, or slip estimation can provide that information. Learning-based policies face the same issue. They may never represent contact as a wrench, but they still need observations or temporal context that distinguish contact states requiring different actions.

That suggests another option: alongside richer abstractions, can we also design the physical interaction itself?

## What CMD designs, changes, and evaluates

There is no universally good contact. One task wants a large compliant patch; another wants a small, localized one. A fingertip may need to be compliant in one direction and stiff in another. Some interactions should resist motion; others should release with almost no resistance. Maximizing friction, compliance, contact area, or grip force alone cannot settle those choices.

CMD starts with physical design variables:

- Contact geometry and material distribution.
- Local stiffness and rigid support configuration.

These choices shape contact responses: how the patch evolves, how pressure is distributed, how material deforms, and how load travels through the structure. Those responses also depend on the object, loading history, and motion. Patch evolution is a response to design and operating conditions, rather than an independently chosen design variable.

At the system level, we evaluate what these responses mean for joint loading, sensor observability, and closed-loop behavior. The distinction tells us what we choose, what changes physically, and what we assess for the task.

**Contact Morphology Design is an approach to choosing contact geometry, material distribution, local stiffness, and structural support to shape contact responses within a robot system.**

## Contact morphology in the closed-loop robot

A soft pad never exists in isolation. Changing its stiffness can change deformation, the contact patch, and the load path through the finger. Those changes can affect joint torque, the mechanical impedance seen at the actuator, sensor response, and the dynamics the controller sees.

Contact morphology therefore has to be studied as part of the closed-loop robot. The same compliant interface can behave differently on a highly geared, stiff actuator and a backdrivable one. A morphology evaluated under position control may behave differently under torque or impedance control. A feature that looks insignificant in a static test can matter once the system moves and the controller closes the loop.

CMD sits between mechanics, hardware design, sensing, and control, and asks two questions:

1. How does physical morphology shape the interaction seen by the complete robot system?
2. How should morphology be designed for the controller, model, sensing, and hardware it will work with?

## Same shape, same motion, different interaction

Three fingertips with identical outer shape and soft material law push back with normal forces more than five times apart under the same indentation. Their largest reconstructed contact area is only about 12% larger than the smallest.

The three share a 30 × 14 mm rounded profile, extruded 12 mm through the section. All use the same Neo-Hookean material law, with $E=100$ kPa and $\nu=0.35$. Their internal structures differ:

- **Hybrid:** a rigid T-shaped insert extends 6 mm into the pad and redirects load. The surrounding soft material is bonded to the insert, with no lateral void.
- **Compliant:** fourteen hexagonal channels form a honeycomb whose walls can deform.
- **Solid:** soft material fills the envelope and is bonded to a rigid backing.

Newton computes each structure under the same platen motions: normal indentation, two loading–release cycles, and lateral translation after preload. Friction is $\mu=0.6$ in every case. The animation connects the internal deformation to the force-bearing contact region and resulting reactions.

At 2.4 mm normal indentation and at the end of the lateral motion, the recorded results are:

| Structure | Normal reaction at 2.4 mm indentation | Reconstructed area at 2.4 mm indentation | Normal / lateral reaction at 1.5 mm preload + 3 mm translation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hybrid | 6.34 N | 201.7 mm² | 3.15 N / −1.87 N |
| Solid | 3.98 N | 193.0 mm² | 1.95 N / −1.17 N |
| Compliant | 1.16 N | 179.3 mm² | 0.55 N / −0.33 N |

Negative lateral reaction opposes the prescribed translation. The lateral case holds indentation equal, giving different normal preload forces across structures.

These simulation results show what individual summaries leave out. Similar total contact areas accompany different forces. Neither an area nor an endpoint force alone says how each pad will respond to the next motion. Here, the internal geometry and support configuration are design variables. The resulting load paths help explain the contact response.

Which structure is useful depends on the task. Limiting force under an uncertain approach favors a lower force rise over the approach range. Carrying load within limited travel asks whether the required force is reached before travel runs out. Either choice needs the full force–displacement response, not just endpoints.

This example isolates the mechanical part of the loop: prescribed motion and a fixed backing, without an actuator or feedback controller. The specimens are illustrative, with no fitted silicone model. The simulation includes numerical damping and omits self-contact after cell collapse. It does not demonstrate reduced policy training cost.

## Can morphology simplify estimation and control?

Contact morphology shapes the physical interaction that sensing and control must handle. Its effects depend on the actuators, transmission, and controller with which it operates. Control changes motion and loading, which in turn change the contact response. CMD therefore treats morphology and control as a co-design problem.

The burden on estimation and control depends on which contact states matter for the task. If similar sensor readings correspond to states requiring different actions, estimation needs additional measurements or history. If the response depends on prior loading, a model or policy may need to track that history. Contact complexity alone does not determine control cost.

**The research hypothesis is that shaping contact responses can make task-relevant behavior more consistent or easier to observe, reducing what estimation and control must represent.** Whether this simplifies policy learning or improves manipulation performance remains to be tested.

For learned manipulation policies, that hypothesis is testable. Hold the task, observation access, policy architecture, and training protocol fixed, change the morphology, and measure:

1. **Sample efficiency:** how much training each design needs to reach a target success rate.
2. **Equal-budget performance:** how each design performs at the same training budget.
3. **Generalization:** how each handles object poses and disturbances outside the training distribution.

One test would use a task requiring repeated support changes: make, shift, release, and re-grasp. The question is whether morphology makes those transitions more reliable while preserving the contact repertoire the task needs, and whether that reliability reduces the required training.

Alongside the control question,

> How should a robot control contact?

CMD asks a design question:

> How should the robot be physically designed so that forces, deformations, sensing, and control produce the interaction we want?
