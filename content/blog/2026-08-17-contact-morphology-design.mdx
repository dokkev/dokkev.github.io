A robot placing a fingertip against an object must tolerate some error in where contact begins. The fingertip's mechanical response determines how that error becomes force. During a sideways motion, the same interface must transmit shear while its contact region changes. Choosing its structure therefore requires knowing what the robot needs to do through contact.

**Contact Morphology Design (CMD) starts with the contact response required by a task. It designs contact geometry, material distribution, and structural support under the robot's sensing and control conditions.** I use CMD to name a research perspective and a way to organize design decisions. Its central question is: what mechanical response should the interface provide so the robot can perform and observe the intended interaction?

## From a task to a contact response

A contact requirement needs a loading path and an operating range. A requirement to limit force during uncertain approach concerns how force grows with indentation. A requirement to transmit a load within limited travel places a different demand on that response. Repeated contact adds recovery; motion along an object adds shear and sliding.

For a small change in normal indentation, a local approximation is

$$
\Delta F_n\approx k_c\Delta\delta,\qquad k_c=\frac{\partial F_n}{\partial\delta},
$$

where $F_n$ is normal force, $\delta$ is indentation, and $k_c$ is the local contact stiffness. The slope depends on the interface's current state. A design requirement can therefore concern an entire force–motion trajectory, including how contact forms and releases.

CMD organizes the design problem around this response:

| Design decision | Question to resolve |
| --- | --- |
| Specify the interaction | What approach, loading, release, and sideways motions must the interface accommodate? |
| Set response targets | What force growth, available travel, contact-region evolution, and recovery are acceptable over those motions? |
| Choose the morphology | How should surface geometry, soft material, voids, and rigid support distribute deformation and load? |
| Evaluate the robot system | Can the available sensors distinguish the required contact states, and can the controller regulate the interaction within its limits? |

Sensing and control constrain the choice from the beginning. Sensor placement determines which part of the mechanical response is observed. Motion resolution and feedback delay affect which force–motion responses the controller can use. Evaluating those dependencies is part of the research problem; the simulation below isolates the mechanical response.

## One outer shape, three internal structures

Two fingertips can use the same soft material and still transform the same motion into different contact forces. An embedded rigid insert redirects load, while internal channels give material room to deform. This example varies those internal structures while holding the outer envelope and soft material law fixed.

- **Hybrid Structure:** a T-shaped rigid carrier extends into the soft pad. The material fills the space around the stem and is bonded to it, with no lateral void.
- **Compliant Structure:** fourteen hexagonal channels form a honeycomb interior. Its walls and outer skin deform above a rigid backing.
- **Solid Structure:** the same outer envelope is filled with soft material and bonded to the backing, without an internal stem.

Internal material volume and mass differ. These are illustrative specimens, with material parameters that have not been fitted to a particular silicone.

Newton computes their deformation under three prescribed platen motions. Each structure receives the same motion in each case, so the resulting forces can differ.

| Case | Prescribed motion | Design question illustrated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal load | Indent from 0 to 2.4 mm over 1.2 s | How does motion become force, and where is that force transmitted? |
| Cyclic load | Two 0 → 2.4 → 0 mm cycles, each lasting 2 s | How does the response change during loading and release? |
| Lateral load | Preload to 1.5 mm, then translate 3 mm sideways | How does the loaded structure respond to shear and redistribute contact? |

## Read the response against the requirement

At the end of normal loading, all specimens have the same 2.4 mm indentation:

| Structure | Normal reaction | Geometric contact-area estimate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hybrid Structure | 6.344 N | 204.9 mm² |
| Compliant Structure | 1.159 N | 182.2 mm² |
| Solid Structure | 3.980 N | 193.3 mm² |

The embedded stem constrains deformation inside the hybrid pad; the honeycomb leaves room for cellular walls to deform. Under this loading condition, the filled pad lies between their normal-force responses.

A requirement to limit force over an uncertain approach would favor a lower force rise over that approach range. A requirement to carry a specified load within limited travel would ask whether the structure reaches that load before exhausting its travel. The endpoint values above cannot settle either choice: both require the response over the relevant motion range. Contact-region evolution adds where the structure transmits load as that motion proceeds.

Loading history also belongs in the requirement. The cyclic case computes loading and return motion. Its friction and numerical damping dissipate energy, so a loading–return difference does not establish a physical material's viscoelasticity or fatigue behavior. A repeated-contact design would need recovery evaluated on its actual cycle and timescale.

The lateral case holds indentation at 1.5 mm and moves the platen sideways. At 3 mm translation:

| Structure | Normal reaction | Lateral reaction | Geometric contact-area estimate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hybrid Structure | 3.152 N | -1.873 N | 161.9 mm² |
| Compliant Structure | 0.554 N | -0.332 N | 140.4 mm² |
| Solid Structure | 1.950 N | -1.167 N | 147.4 mm² |

Negative lateral reaction opposes the platen's positive translation. The shared friction coefficient means these differences cannot be attributed to different surface friction properties. Normal preload forces and deformation differ across the structures. For a task requiring shear transmission, the next comparison must specify whether indentation or normal force is held equal. The contact view alone does not classify sticking and sliding.

## Close the design loop with sensing and control

The remaining question is whether a chosen mechanical response helps the robot execute its task. For force regulation, that requires evaluating the structure together with the controller across the intended contact range. For tactile sensing, it requires checking whether loads and contact locations produce distinguishable sensor outputs through the chosen structure.

The animation contains prescribed motions and mechanical responses; it does not evaluate a sensor or a feedback controller. A CMD study must connect response targets to a morphology choice, then test that choice under the intended sensing and control conditions. For these specimens, that means defining an approach or shear task and selecting its force and travel limits. Candidate structures then need evaluation with the same robot and sensing arrangement.
