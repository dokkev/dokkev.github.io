The harmonic drive is a reducer widely used in the joints of collaborative robots and precision robot arms. The name is a product name that stuck; the generic term is strain wave gear. It is often introduced as "the reducer with no backlash," but having no backlash does not mean the output follows the input exactly. A harmonic drive nearly eliminates clearance between tooth flanks, but in exchange it leaves other errors that come from the elastic deformation of a thin metal part.

This post briefly summarizes the structure of the harmonic drive and then looks at four errors it leaves in a joint. The general costs of reducers (reflected inertia, friction, stiffness, and lost motion) were covered in the [parent post](/hardware03/).

## Structure and Gear Ratio

A harmonic drive consists of three parts.

1. **Wave generator:** the input element, an elliptical cam fitted with a thin bearing. It connects to the motor shaft.
2. **Flexspline:** a thin, cup-shaped metal gear with external teeth. The wave generator deforms it into an ellipse, and it is usually connected to the output shaft.
3. **Circular spline:** a rigid ring gear with internal teeth. It is usually fixed to the housing and has two more teeth than the flexspline.

As the wave generator turns, the flexspline and circular spline mesh at the two ends of the ellipse's major axis, and the mesh location rotates with the input. For each turn of the wave generator, the flexspline shifts in the opposite direction by the tooth difference, that is, by two teeth. With the circular spline fixed and the flexspline as the output, the gear ratio is

$$
N = \frac{Z_{fs}}{Z_{cs} - Z_{fs}}
$$

$Z_{fs}$ and $Z_{cs}$ are the tooth counts of the flexspline and the circular spline. With $Z_{fs}=200$ and $Z_{cs}=202$, $N=100$, and the output turns opposite to the input. The main advantages of this structure are a large ratio in a single stage, and a shorter axial length and lower weight than a multi-stage planetary gearbox of the same ratio.

Near the major axis, several pairs of teeth mesh at once, and the flexspline is assembled pressed toward the circular spline. So backlash, the geometric clearance between tooth flanks, is close to zero. The errors below come from this structure.

## Error 1: Stiffness That Changes with Torque

The torsional stiffness of a harmonic drive is not a constant. It is lowest near zero torque and rises as torque increases. Manufacturer catalogs also give stiffness not as a single value but split by torque range, as $K_1$, $K_2$, $K_3$. A measured torque–twist curve is shallow near the origin and gets steeper farther out [1](#ref-1), [2](#ref-2).

The trouble is that the lowest-stiffness range is near zero torque. The joint is softest exactly when the robot is holding light contact or when torque reverses, that is, when precise force control is needed. The resonant frequency $\omega_r \propto \sqrt{K_s}$ from the [parent post](/hardware03/) is also lowest in this range, so a controller designed with a single stiffness value may run into a lower resonance than expected in the low-torque range.

## Error 2: Hysteresis in Place of Backlash

If torque is raised in one direction and then brought down in the other, the torque–twist curve of a harmonic drive does not return along the same path; it traces a loop. This comes from tooth flank friction and internal losses in the flexspline. Catalogs list this separately as hysteresis loss, and the position loss when the input reverses in the low-torque range as lost motion.

So "zero backlash" should be read as "no geometric clearance." The output position not following the input exactly when torque reverses still exists in this reducer. But because the cause is elasticity and friction rather than a gap, it appears not as a range where the output is fully disconnected but as a torque–position relation that depends on history. How to distinguish backlash from hysteresis in a model is covered in detail in [the post on backlash](/hardware05/).

## Error 3: Transmission Error Repeating Twice per Input Revolution

Even under constant load, the output position of a harmonic drive deviates periodically from the ideal $\theta_m/N$. The largest component of this transmission error repeats twice per revolution of the wave generator, because the ellipse has two ends on its major axis and the flexspline deforms twice per input revolution. Out-of-roundness of the wave generator, tooth profile errors in the flexspline and circular spline, and assembly eccentricity produce errors at this period [1](#ref-1).

This error shows up in data in two ways.

- **Position error:** with only a motor encoder, this error is invisible. With an output-side encoder, the difference between motor position and output position retains a component at twice the input rotation frequency.
- **Vibration and torque ripple:** transmission error acts as a periodic displacement input to the joint. When the motor reaches a speed at which this frequency coincides with the joint's resonant frequency, vibration grows. Cases where a harmonic drive joint shakes only at certain speeds fall into this category [1](#ref-1).

Because this error is tied to input position, modeling it as random noise over time does not fit. It can be compensated if measured as a function of position, but its magnitude can change with load and temperature.

## Error 4: Friction That Varies Widely with Load and Temperature

In a harmonic drive, several pairs of teeth mesh while sliding, and the wave generator bearing rotates while continuously deforming. So efficiency varies widely with input speed, load torque, and lubricant temperature. In general, efficiency drops the further load falls below rated and the colder the lubricant. Manufacturer catalogs give this as efficiency curves for each condition and as no-load running torque. There are also experimental results showing that an accurate dynamic model must include this nonlinear friction [1](#ref-1).

Backdriving is possible, but the backdrive starting torque is large. Because of this, in a harmonic drive joint the torque computed from motor current can differ substantially from the actual joint torque. The error is especially large in the low-torque range, where friction takes a larger share. Many collaborative robots that need to comply with external forces avoid this problem by adding a separate joint torque sensor on the harmonic drive output shaft.

## Load Limits: Ratcheting and Flexspline Fatigue

The load limits of a harmonic drive are set by the flexspline.

- **Ratcheting:** under a load above the momentary peak torque, the flexspline teeth can ride over the circular spline teeth. If a tooth skips, the output position shifts, and repeated skipping damages the teeth. This needs care in joints that see large impacts, such as collisions or landings.
- **Fatigue life:** the flexspline deforms twice for every input revolution. With the motor turning at thousands of rpm, deformation cycles accumulate quickly, so the flexspline is designed to withstand high-cycle fatigue. Prolonged operation above rated torque shortens its life quickly.

As shown in the [parent post](/hardware03/), in a high-ratio joint the reducer must transmit the torque that stops the rotor in a collision. Because a harmonic drive has a high ratio, this load is large, so designs sometimes detect collisions with an output-side torque sensor or add a deliberate elastic element, as in an SEA, to reduce peak load.

## What to Keep in the Model

| Harmonic drive characteristic | What a simple model leaves out | How to handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Torque-dependent stiffness | A single stiffness constant, or a rigid joint | Check resonance with the per-range stiffness values, and set gains conservatively in the low-torque range |
| Hysteresis and lost motion | $q=\theta_m/N$ | Use an output-side encoder; include the range of position error during direction reversal in the model |
| Transmission error repeating twice per input revolution | Random noise, or ignored | Measure as a function of position and compensate; check speed ranges that coincide with resonance |
| Load- and temperature-dependent friction | Constant friction or a single efficiency | Identify friction per joint; consider a joint torque sensor for the low-torque range |
| Ratcheting torque | A single torque limit | Compare peak torque during collisions and landings with the momentary peak torque |
