In robotics research, however, the word "hardware" is used far more broadly. "Doing hardware" usually means running experiments on a real robot rather than in simulation, and that covers almost everything needed to make the robot move reliably: the microcontrollers and embedded systems that control the motors at high frequency, the network setup for communicating with the control PC, and the basic controllers, such as gravity compensation and posture control, that let the robot support and hold itself up.

Strictly speaking, much of this is software. We still group it under hardware because, without it, the robot never even reaches a state where experiments or data collection are possible. In robotics research, then, the test for hardware is less "is it physical?" and more "is it essential to running the real robot?"

So if you think of robot hardware as just "a machine with a lot of motors," you miss something important. From the Physical AI perspective that is now drawing so much attention, a robot is less a machine that executes commands and more an intelligent system that interacts directly with the physical world. The robot applies forces to the environment, the environment changes under those forces, and the robot senses that change again through its sensors. Hardware is always at the center of this interaction.

Multi-joint robots may look alike, but the way they actually interact with the environment differs greatly from one piece of hardware to another. Two joints with the same apparent structure can have different inertia and friction at the output depending on the reducer type and ratio, and that determines how easily the joint is pushed back by external forces, that is, its **backdrivability**. In particular, the motor rotor's inertia appears at the output scaled by the square of the reduction ratio (reflected inertia), so even with the same motor, a 100:1 joint has 100 times more reflected inertia from the rotor than a 10:1 joint. Inertia, friction, and stiffness together form the **mechanical impedance**, which governs how stiffly or softly the robot responds at the moment of contact.

Add the mass distribution and inertia of the whole robot, and the same control input can produce very different forces and accelerations. In tasks with repeated contact, such as manipulation and locomotion, these differences translate directly into system stability and task success rate.

The same goes for sensing and control. Control rate, communication latency and noise, and sensor sensitivity, linearity, and hysteresis all have a large effect on control performance. Add structural stiffness, joint stiffness, friction, and heat, and the interaction a robot produces becomes the combined result of many mechanical, electromagnetic, and thermal properties.

## AI and Robot Hardware

The complexity of robot hardware is more than a mechanical design problem. It becomes a fundamental barrier when applying modern AI algorithms to real robots. This barrier shows up in two main forms: the Sim-to-Real Gap and the Embodiment Gap.

The first is the Sim-to-Real Gap. It stands out in reinforcement learning, where policies are trained in simulation, and it is a core challenge not only for legged robots and drone control but also for manipulation, such as in-hand manipulation with robot hands [20](#ref-20). Simulation cannot perfectly reproduce subtle physical properties such as friction, gear backlash, structural flexibility, and communication latency. When a policy overfits to the idealized physics of the simulator, it often becomes unstable or behaves strangely on the real robot at the slightest friction or vibration.

Interestingly, one effective way to close this gap is to model the hardware itself more accurately. In the ANYmal legged robot work, for example, an **actuator network** trained on input-output data from the real actuators was placed in the simulator, and the policy trained in simulation transferred successfully to the real robot [21](#ref-21). The forces a robot exchanges with its environment are ultimately determined by the physical properties of the real hardware, not by the simulator.

The second is the Embodiment Gap. The term can also refer to the gap when transferring human demonstrations to robots (human-to-robot), but here it means the differences between robots. It is the first problem you hit when scaling **data-driven approaches** that train a **policy** on large amounts of data, that is, VLA models or imitation learning, across multiple robots.

Kinematic differences such as link lengths and degrees of freedom can be compensated to some extent by **retargeting**. Of course, when robots differ a lot in degrees of freedom, thumb placement, and contact locations, as robot hands do, retargeting itself is still a hard problem. The more fundamental issue, though, is that each robot has different dynamics and a different relationship to force. Even when tracking the same trajectory, the force a robot applies to the environment and the reaction it receives vary widely with joint friction, actuator inertia, reducer backlash, and control bandwidth.

Take a grasp in which the target position is set 1 cm inside the object's surface. A **compliant** robot under low-stiffness impedance control squeezes the object with a force of roughly stiffness × position error in a quasi-static linear approximation, but a **stiff** robot under high-gain position control applies a much larger force for the same command. The problem is that many imitation learning and VLA datasets today record actions as position targets. Force is not recorded explicitly in the data; it is buried implicitly in the impedance characteristics of the hardware that collected the data.

So when the hardware platform changes, the same action produces a different physical outcome, and **transferring** collected data and trained policies to another robot becomes difficult. Efforts such as Open X-Embodiment, which train on data pooled from many robots, have shown some positive transfer [22](#ref-22), but the more contact-rich the task, the bigger an obstacle these force differences inevitably become.

In the end, the limits of AI-based robot control often come less from the algorithms themselves than from the physical conditions the hardware imposes. Without a deep understanding of hardware, it is hard to close the Sim-to-Real Gap, and hard to build general-purpose datasets that bridge the Embodiment Gap.

## Understanding Robot Hardware

In this blog, I plan to cover robot hardware layer by layer. Starting with actuators such as motors and reducers, then sensors, and then low-level control and physics-model-based control such as gravity compensation, we will walk step by step through what a multi-joint robot needs in order to actually work.

For each layer, we will look at what role it plays, where its limits arise, and how those limits affect the performance of real robots and AI-based control. The goal is to organize this material from the perspective of someone who has to design, control, and troubleshoot robots, not just use them.
