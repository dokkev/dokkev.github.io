The planetary gearbox is used across the widest range of any reducer, from the QDD actuators of legged robots to industrial servo reducers. It is built entirely from rigid gears, so it has no thin elastic part like a harmonic drive, and its single-stage ratio is low, which makes it easy to backdrive. In exchange, rigid gears need a gap between tooth flanks to mesh, and the number of teeth in mesh keeps changing as they rotate.

This post summarizes the structure and gear ratio of a planetary gearbox and then looks at the errors this structure leaves in a joint. The general costs of reducers were covered in the [parent post](/hardware03/).

## Structure and Gear Ratio

A planetary gearbox consists of four elements.

1. **Sun gear:** the external gear at the center, usually the input.
2. **Planet gears:** several gears that rotate while meshing between the sun gear and the ring gear.
3. **Ring gear:** the internal gear surrounding the outside, usually fixed to the housing.
4. **Carrier:** the element that holds the planet gear axles, usually the output.

With the ring gear fixed, the sun gear as input, and the carrier as output, the gear ratio is

$$
N = \frac{\omega_{sun}}{\omega_{carrier}} = 1 + \frac{Z_r}{Z_s}
$$

$Z_r$ and $Z_s$ are the tooth counts of the ring gear and the sun gear. With $Z_s=20$ and $Z_r=80$, $N=5$, and the output turns in the same direction as the input. For the three gears to mesh with the same module, $Z_r = Z_s + 2Z_p$ must hold ($Z_p$ is the planet gear tooth count), and for the planet gears to be evenly spaced, $(Z_s + Z_r)$ must be divisible by the number of planet gears.

### A Small Single-Stage Ratio

To raise the ratio, the sun gear has to get smaller or the ring gear larger. If the sun gear gets too small, the tooth roots weaken and there is no room for the input shaft; enlarging the ring gear increases the reducer's diameter. So a single stage ranges roughly from 3:1 to 10:1. When a larger ratio is needed, several stages are stacked in series.

This constraint also turns into an advantage. As shown in the [parent post](/hardware03/), reflected inertia scales with $N^2$ and motor-side friction with $N$, so both costs are small at a low single-stage ratio. The MIT Mini Cheetah actuator combines an outrunner motor with a 6:1 single-stage planetary gearbox to make a backdrivable joint [2](#ref-2). This configuration is covered in detail in [the QDD post](/hardware04/).

### Load Sharing Among Planet Gears

With $n$ planet gears, each ideally transmits $1/n$ of the total torque. The tooth flank load is smaller than in a single gear pair of the same size, so large torque can be transmitted in a small volume, and impact loads are also split across several tooth flanks. This sharing is the ideal case, though; as shown below, in practice it is not even.

## Error 1: Backlash

For rigid gears to mesh without binding, a gap is needed between tooth flanks. Given machining errors, shaft and bearing eccentricity, thermal expansion, and the lubricant film, this gap cannot be made zero. A planetary gearbox has two kinds of mesh, sun–planet and planet–ring, and the gaps at each mesh combine with the clearance of the carrier bearings to form the backlash at the output shaft.

Stacking stages accumulates backlash, but not every stage contributes equally. Seen from the output shaft, clearance in an earlier stage appears divided by the ratio of the later stage. The output backlash of a two-stage reducer is roughly

$$
b_{out} \approx b_2 + \frac{b_1}{N_2}
$$

$b_1$ and $b_2$ are the backlash of each stage expressed as an angle at that stage's output shaft, and $N_2$ is the ratio of the second stage. So output backlash is usually dominated by the last stage, and precision reducers put their effort into reducing the clearance of the last stage.

Backlash disconnects the motor from the output shaft at the moment torque reverses. Examples are a legged robot's leg going from swing to touchdown, or passing through a configuration where the gravity torque changes sign. The resulting state mismatch and external force estimation problems are covered in detail in [the post on backlash](/hardware05/).

## Error 2: Stiffness That Changes Every Mesh Period

As a spur gear rotates, the number of tooth pairs in simultaneous mesh alternates between one and two. With two pairs in mesh, the load is shared and stiffness is high; with only one pair, stiffness is low. So the torsional stiffness of a planetary gearbox is not a constant but varies repeatedly every mesh period.

In the fixed-ring configuration, the mesh frequency is the product of the ring gear tooth count and the carrier rotation rate:

$$
f_{mesh} = Z_r \cdot \frac{\omega_{carrier}}{2\pi}
$$

Stiffness variation at this frequency produces periodic transmission error and vibration even under constant load. Add eccentricity and tooth profile errors in each gear, and components also appear at the carrier rotation frequency and the planet gear spin frequency. In data, this shows up as torque ripple and noise at frequencies proportional to joint speed.

Helical gears have teeth cut at an angle so that several tooth pairs engage gradually. More teeth are in mesh at once, which reduces stiffness variation and noise. In exchange, the tooth angle produces an axial load that requires bearings to support it, and double-helical designs that cancel the axial load are hard to machine.

## Error 3: Uneven Load Sharing Among Planet Gears

Because of machining and assembly errors, the planet gears do not split the load exactly $1/n$ each. A planet gear with a position error makes contact first and takes more load, and the rest only share the load after it grows enough to cause elastic deformation. As a result, in the low-torque range only some of the gears transmit load, so stiffness is low, and as torque increases more gears share the load and stiffness rises.

This unevenness also lowers fatigue life and the impact limit. Rated torque is set more conservatively than a value assuming even sharing would allow, and some designs let the sun gear float radially so that it equalizes the load on its own.

## Efficiency and Backdrivability

Rigid gear mesh is mostly close to rolling motion, so single-stage efficiency is fairly high. Losses multiply as stages are stacked, and friction in an earlier stage appears at the output shaft amplified by the ratio of the later stages. So even within the same planetary structure, a single-stage 6:1 backdrives easily by hand, while a three-stage 100:1 is much harder to backdrive. Backdrivability depends more on the number of stages and the overall ratio than on the type of gear.

## What to Keep in the Model

| Planetary gear characteristic | What a simple model leaves out | How to handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Backlash (dominated by the last stage) | $q=\theta_m/N$ | Output-side encoder; model the state mismatch during direction reversal |
| Mesh-period stiffness variation | Rigid joint | Measure as a periodic disturbance proportional to joint speed; check speeds that coincide with resonance |
| Uneven load sharing | Even sharing | Low stiffness in the low-torque range; check the margin on the rating |
| Friction depending on the number of stages | A single efficiency | Measure backdrive torque based on the number of stages and the overall ratio |
