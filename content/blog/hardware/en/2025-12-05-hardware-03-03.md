A cycloidal drive is a mechanism in which a disc in eccentric motion rolls along pins in the housing to reduce speed. It gets a large ratio in a single stage, and because several lobes share the load at once, it withstands impacts well. The RV reducers in industrial robots use this principle, and it has recently been used in legged robots and small actuators as well.

A cycloidal disc with the theoretical profile touches every pin with no gap, but machined exactly that way it would bind because of assembly tolerances and thermal expansion. So real discs have their profile slightly trimmed, and this modification creates the errors specific to cycloidal reducers. This post summarizes the structure and gear ratio and then looks at those errors. The general costs of reducers were covered in the [parent post](/hardware03/).

## Structure and Gear Ratio

A cycloidal reducer works in three steps.

1. **Eccentric input:** an eccentric cam on the motor shaft rotates and moves the center of the disc along a small circular orbit.
2. **Disc motion:** the lobes on the outside of the disc mesh with the ring pins in the housing, so the disc slowly spins in the opposite direction while it orbits.
3. **Output extraction:** output pins sit in holes in the disc, filtering out the orbital motion and passing only the slow spin to the output shaft.

### Gear Ratio by Fixed Element

Let $Z_p$ be the number of ring pins and $Z_d = Z_p - 1$ the number of lobes on the disc.

- **Housing fixed, disc as output:** for each turn of the input, the disc spins by one lobe in the direction opposite to the input. The gear ratio is $N = Z_d/(Z_p - Z_d) = Z_p - 1$.

Increasing the number of pins gives a single-stage ratio of tens to one or more, so the reducer can be made axially shorter than a multi-stage planetary gearbox of the same ratio.

### Load Sharing Across Lobes

Whereas a spur gear transmits torque through one or two tooth pairs, a cycloidal disc with the theoretical profile can have nearly half of its lobes on the torque-transmitting side in contact with pins at once. Because the load is split across many contact points, it withstands large torques and impacts for its size. This is the main reason cycloidal reducers are used in legged robot joints that take repeated landing impacts. As shown below, the actual number of contacts drops with profile modification and load.

## Error 1: Clearance from Profile Modification

A disc machined to the exact theoretical cycloid has zero gap to the pins. Even slight machining error, pin position error, or thermal expansion makes such a disc bind and stop turning, so real discs undergo profile modification: the profile is offset uniformly inward or the pin circle radius is adjusted.

This modification creates a deliberate gap between pins and lobes. If the gap is small, binding can occur depending on tolerances; if it is large, backlash grows and position accuracy drops. Unlike the "zero backlash" of a harmonic drive, the backlash of a cycloidal reducer is a value the designer sets through the amount of modification.

## Error 2: Contact Count and Stiffness That Grow with Load

With a modified profile, only a few lobes touch the pins under no load. As torque increases, the disc and pins deform elastically and more lobes come into contact. So stiffness is low in the low-torque range and rises with torque, and the torque–twist curve is shallow near the origin, as with a harmonic drive.

The consequences are also the same: the joint is softest in the low-torque range where precise force control is needed, and a single stiffness value cannot predict resonance accurately. Backlash and the deformation in the low-torque range together make up the lost motion when the input reverses.

## Error 3: Clearance at the Output Pins

The output pin mechanism that takes the disc's spin out to the output shaft also adds clearance. Because the output pins have to roll along an eccentric orbit inside the disc holes, each hole must be larger than its pin by twice the eccentricity, plus a gap for machining tolerance. This gap adds to lost motion separately from the clearance between ring pins and lobes, so reducing the backlash of a cycloidal reducer means managing both places together.

## Error 4: Vibration from the Eccentric Mass

The disc's center of mass travels once around the eccentric orbit for each input revolution. As a result, a centrifugal force at the input rotation frequency acts on the housing, growing with the square of input speed. To cancel it, two discs 180° out of phase are often stacked. Their centrifugal forces cancel, but because the two discs are separated axially, a moment proportional to that distance remains. Designs that use three discs or add counterweights reduce this moment as well, at the cost of more weight and length.

In a high-ratio joint where the motor runs fast, this vibration can coincide with the joint's resonance. As with the transmission error of a harmonic drive, you can confirm in the data that the vibration frequency is tied to motor speed.

## Friction by Contact Type

How the pins and lobes make contact changes efficiency and backdrivability considerably.

- **Rolling contact:** rollers or bearings are fitted on the ring pins and output pins so that the pins rotate. Friction is low, so efficiency is high and backdriving is easy, but the part count goes up and it is hard to increase the number of pins, which works against miniaturization.
- **Sliding contact:** the disc meshes by sliding over fixed pins. The structure is simple and can be made small, but friction and wear are high and efficiency is low. To reduce sliding friction, discs are sometimes made from low-friction plastics such as POM (Delrin) or nylon, but these have lower stiffness and allowable load than metal.

In practice, bearings go on the eccentric cam, which carries the largest load, and the pin contacts are chosen as a balance between size and efficiency. Even at the same ratio, backdrive torque varies widely with contact type, so the backdrivability of a cycloidal reducer cannot be judged from its ratio alone.

## What to Keep in the Model

| Cycloidal reducer characteristic | What a simple model leaves out | How to handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clearance from profile modification and output pins | $q=\theta_m/N$ | Output-side encoder; measure lost motion during direction reversal |
| Load-dependent stiffness | A single stiffness constant | Measure stiffness per torque range; check gains in the low-torque range |
| Vibration from the eccentric mass | Ignored | Measure vibration at the input rotation frequency and check speeds that coincide with resonance |
| Friction depending on contact type | A single efficiency | Identify backdrive torque and friction per joint |
| Load sharing across lobes | A single torque limit | Check the impact load limit separately from rated torque |
